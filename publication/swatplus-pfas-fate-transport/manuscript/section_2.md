## 3.1 High-resolution model generation and discretization

### Automated model generation

The watershed model was generated by the SWATGenX platform, an automated pipeline that assembles a fully parameterized SWAT+ model for any drainage in the conterminous United States directly from national geospatial archives, with no manual delineation or hand-editing of inputs. For the Huron River watershed (HUC8 04100013) the pipeline ingested five primary data sources: the National Hydrography Dataset Plus High Resolution (NHDPlus HR) for the stream network and catchment topology; the 1/3 arc-second (~10 m) 3D Elevation Program (3DEP) digital elevation model for terrain and flow-direction conditioning; the gridded Soil Survey Geographic Database (gSSURGO) for soil horizons and their physical and chemical properties; the National Land Cover Database (NLCD) together with the USDA Cropland Data Layer (CDL) for land use and management, rasterized at 250 m; and Parameter-elevation Regressions on Independent Slopes Model (PRISM) fields for daily precipitation and temperature forcing. The hydrography drives the channel network, the conditioned 3DEP DEM supplies slopes and flow routing, and the gSSURGO–NLCD/CDL intersection defines the hydrologic response units (HRUs) that carry the land-phase mass balance.

The resulting model discretizes the Huron watershed into **72,475 HRUs**, **3,119 stream reaches (channels)**, approximately **3,430 routing units**, and roughly **90 reservoirs**. Each HRU is a unique land use–soil–slope combination within a routing unit and is the elementary control volume on which the land-phase water, sediment, and PFAS balances are solved; each channel is an explicit NHDPlus HR flowline carrying the in-stream balance. The serial engine requires approximately **2.85 GB** peak memory and roughly **13 minutes per simulated year**, placing multi-decadal, ensemble-based calibration within reach of a parallelized, cloud-dispatched workflow (Section 3.5). Because every input is a national-coverage archive and every processing step is scripted, the same generation procedure transfers to any HUC8 in the conterminous United States without re-tooling — a reproducibility property we return to in the Discussion rather than claim as a core scientific advance.

### Why reach-scale resolution is a methodological advance, not a cosmetic refinement

This discretization represents an approximately **16.5× increase in stream-network resolution** over the authors' prior distributed model of the same watershed (~189 reaches; Rafiei & Nejadhashemi 2023) **[VERIFY the 189-reach denominator against the 2023 paper's reported discretization before printing the 16.5× ratio; until verified, state "approximately an order of magnitude"]**. The gain is consequential for PFOS transport for three reasons, each anchored in the established discretization-sensitivity and drainage-area-transfer literature; we state proactively that the advance is in *network geometry and observation assignment*, not in the resolution of meteorological forcing or in the dimensionality of the parameter vector.

First, **PFOS belongs to the resolution-sensitive constituent class.** Watershed-subdivision studies consistently find that simulated streamflow is largely insensitive to subbasin count, whereas sediment- and particulate-associated constituents are strongly sensitive: Jha et al. (2004) show that stabilizing sediment loads requires subbasin drainage areas below roughly 3% of total watershed area (about 2% for nitrate and 5% for phosphorus), and FitzHugh and Mackay (2000) show that the sediment response to coarsening is systematically biased rather than merely noisy, propagating through a nonlinear source-area relationship. Because PFOS partitions strongly to organic carbon and sediment (Section 3.2), it is governed by the same physics that places sediment in the resolution-sensitive class; the applicable discretization standard is therefore the ~2–5% sediment/nutrient threshold, which a 189-reach delineation cannot satisfy and a 3,119-reach delineation can.

Second, **the resolution gain is inherited from the input hydrography and restores headwater reaches that coarser networks omit.** NHDPlus HR is built on 1:24,000-scale (or finer) hydrography conditioned to the 1/3 arc-second 3DEP DEM, against the 1:100,000-scale basis of the medium-resolution NHDPlus V2 (Moore et al. 2019). Coarser networks do not merely lump headwaters; they delete them — and first-order streams are both the most abundant channel type and disproportionate sites of in-stream processing owing to their high benthic-area-to-volume ratios (Alexander et al. 2007). The finer network reinstates these reaches as explicit modeling units, so the added resolution is data-supported rather than an arbitrary modeling choice.

Third, and most central to this study, the fine network underpins a **closest-drainage-area observation-to-channel assignment** (detailed in Section 3.4). Every PFAS field measurement is collected at a real upstream drainage area; assigning it to a model channel introduces a representativeness error that scales with the mismatch between the sample's true contributing area and that of the assigned reach. Drainage-area-ratio (DAR) transfer theory establishes that such error grows monotonically as the area ratio departs from unity, with reliable transfer commonly bounded near a 0.5–1.5 ratio (Asquith et al. 2006), and Farmer (2018) shows explicitly that higher network resolution reduces this representativeness error. At 189 reaches an observation is forced onto a coarse aggregate that integrates tributaries it never sampled; at 3,119 reaches the nearest channel by drainage area is a far tighter match, drawing the assignment ratio toward unity so that each simulated reach concentration is evaluated against an observation that genuinely represents that reach. We caution, however, that the DAR validity bands were derived for transferring *streamflow* between hydrologically similar catchments, whereas we use closest drainage area as a station-to-reach *snapping* criterion; we invoke DAR theory only as the conceptual scale of acceptable area mismatch and the principle that representativeness error grows with departure from unity, not as a numerically transferable validity band.

### Costs and limits, stated explicitly

A finer network carries genuine costs, which we manage rather than dismiss. The most common objection — over-parameterization and the equifinality it invites — is substantially blunted by the SWAT+ parameter structure: transport parameters are assigned by class (land use, soil, and source type), not independently per reach, so moving from 189 to 3,119 reaches raises the spatial resolution of *prediction* far faster than the dimension of the *calibration vector*. We further constrain calibration to physically grouped parameters and apply global (Morris) sensitivity screening to fix non-influential parameters before fitting (Section 3.5); equifinality is thereby *managed*, not eliminated. A second limit is forcing-resolution mismatch: we claim no sub-reach certainty in meteorology or in the PFAS source inventory, and we treat those coarser inputs as the limiting resolution for source-attribution confidence. Third, we are not over-resolving past numerical convergence — the Jha et al. thresholds place the 189-reach model on the non-converged side and the present model on the converged side of the sediment/nutrient stability criterion. Finally, the added compute cost is real but tractable through parallelization and cloud-dispatched calibration; it is an engineering cost, justified by the reach-scale decisions (the 4 ng/L MCL, the 50 ppb fish advisory) the model is built to inform, not a scientific barrier.

To convert the central assignment argument from a mechanistic claim into a measured one specific to the Huron PFAS network, we plan a diagnostic figure (Fig. R2) comparing, for the actual ambient stations, the histogram of |log(DAR)| = |log(A_station / A_assigned reach)| under the 189-reach and 3,119-reach networks, together with the fraction of stations falling inside the 0.5–1.5 area-ratio band under each — the single highest-payoff quantification of the resolution advantage and a direct, reviewer-checkable test of whether the finer network materially improves observation representativeness.
