# A high-resolution, process-based PFAS fate-and-transport model of the Huron River watershed (SWAT+): reach-resolved surface-water transport for the post-source-control, sediment-legacy regime

## Abstract

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) in surface waters have entered a regime of enforceable numeric limits—a 2024 U.S. drinking-water Maximum Contaminant Level (MCL) of 4 ng/L for PFOA and PFOS, a CERCLA hazardous-substance designation, and Michigan in-stream Rule 57 values—shifting the management question from detection toward reach-scale prediction, source attribution, and counterfactual forecasting. These are tasks that monitoring and statistical occurrence models, by construction, cannot supply. The need is sharpened in the Huron River watershed (southeastern Michigan), where, following upstream source control, water-column PFOS fell by roughly 99% while fish-tissue PFOS plateaued above consumption-advisory thresholds—a divergence attributed to contaminated sediment acting as a secondary internal source. We present a process-based PFAS surface-water fate-and-transport model of this watershed, generated in SWAT+ by the SWATGenX platform from NHDPlus HR hydrography, 3DEP elevation, gSSURGO soils, and gridded land-use and climate forcings. The model resolves the network into 3,119 channels and 72,475 hydrologic response units—approximately an order-of-magnitude increase in reach resolution over the prior distributed model of the same watershed—allowing each PFAS observation to be assigned to its hydrologically correct channel by closest drainage area. The PFAS engine ports a soil three-phase equilibrium formulation into the modern SWAT+ constituent host, routing PFOS through surface runoff, lateral flow, leaching, and sediment-bound pathways. We describe the model generation, the PFAS process formulation, the soil-PFAS initialization, and the observation-to-channel assignment, and we lay out the calibration and validation design; flow and PFAS calibration results are forthcoming.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Regulatory and management drivers

Over the 2024–2026 period, per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) in surface waters crossed from an emerging contaminant of interest into a regime of enforceable numeric limits, and it is precisely within that regime that source-resolved watershed modeling becomes a decision tool rather than an academic exercise. In April 2024 the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency finalized the first National Primary Drinking Water Regulation for PFAS, establishing legally enforceable Maximum Contaminant Levels (MCLs) of 4.0 ng/L for perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and perfluorooctane sulfonate (PFOS) individually, with 10 ng/L limits for PFHxS, PFNA, and HFPO-DA and a Hazard Index for their mixtures; the agency's 2025 reconsideration retained the PFOA and PFOS MCLs (EPA 2024a; EPA 2025) [VERIFY current deadline status; the 2025 reconsideration adjusted some compliance timelines but retained the PFOA/PFOS MCLs]. A 4 ng/L threshold sits near the analytical detection floor, which means that the source waters feeding drinking-water intakes must be characterized at trace levels—a watershed-loading question, not a treatment-plant question alone. In the same year, EPA's designation of PFOA and PFOS as hazardous substances under the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) introduced strict, joint-and-several liability whose allocation is fundamentally a fate-and-transport attribution problem: which release reached which receptor, by which pathway, and in what proportion (EPA 2024b). Under Clean Water Act §303(d), moreover, any PFAS impairment listing triggers a Total Maximum Daily Load—by statutory definition a load-allocation calculation across point and nonpoint sources, a mechanism now being activated with Michigan as an early exemplar rather than as established nationwide practice. Michigan's surface-water program already operationalizes in-stream numeric criteria through its Rule 57 values (PFOS 12 ng/L, PFOA 11 ng/L), which provide the cleanest regulatory hook of all: a model that predicts reach-resolved in-stream concentration outputs the same quantity that the regulation constrains.

### 1.2 Why monitoring and machine learning are insufficient

These drivers expose capabilities that monitoring, by construction, cannot supply, and the gap is one of capability rather than of goodness-of-fit. First, **source attribution**: a grab sample integrates surface runoff, lateral flow, groundwater discharge, and sediment-bound transport, each with distinct spatial origins, so that only a model separately routing each pathway can disaggregate an observed concentration back to its contributing sources—the basis for CERCLA cost allocation and cleanup prioritization (Rafiei & Nejadhashemi 2023). Second, **prediction in ungauged reaches**: PFAS monitoring is structurally sparse, because national efforts such as EPA's National Rivers and Streams Assessment are probability-based statistical surveys rather than reach censuses, and a global synthesis of more than 45,000 samples indicates that monitoring systematically underestimates true burden owing to limited analyte suites (Ackerman Grunfeld et al. 2024). A calibrated process model is the only mechanism that yields a mass-conserving concentration at every reach of a network, consistent with the long-standing program on prediction in ungauged basins (Sivapalan et al. 2003) [VERIFY]. Third, **counterfactual evaluation**: management decisions are prospective and interventional—removing an upstream source, altering biosolids application, imposing a wasteload allocation—and an intervention acts on a *pathway*, not directly on a concentration, so counterfactual validity requires a model that encodes the correct causal structure.

Machine-learning occurrence models are complementary to, but cannot substitute for, this causal tier. Statistical models predict where PFAS are likely to be detected and are valuable for national-scale triage (Tokranov et al. 2024), but they are non-causal and cannot represent the source-control or sediment-remediation interventions that constitute the regulator's actual levers (Prosperi et al. 2020). We therefore frame the distinction deliberately as capability rather than predictive fit: we do not claim that a process model interpolates observed concentrations better than a well-trained statistical model, nor that it replaces monitoring. Rather, the process model is the inference and forecasting layer that sits atop monitoring and supplies mechanism, mass balance, and counterfactuals—quantities the alternatives structurally cannot reach.

### 1.3 The Huron sediment-legacy problem and prior work

A fourth driver is the scientific centerpiece of this work, because it is a published, watershed-specific observation that static, steady-state, or source-control-only reasoning cannot explain. Following granular-activated-carbon source control at an upstream discharger in the Huron River system, water-column PFOS in the Huron/Kent Lake reach fell by roughly 99%, yet fish-tissue PFOS plateaued *above* the consumption-advisory threshold rather than continuing to decline, with partition coefficients, bioaccumulation factors, and water–sediment–biota patterns consistent with contaminated sediment acting as an ongoing secondary internal source (Endicott et al. 2025). A plateau above threshold despite near-complete external-source removal is the signature of a slowly releasing internal reservoir whose desorption and resuspension timescale outlasts the intervention—a phenomenon that equilibrium or source-inventory accounting cannot reproduce, and that only a time-dependent model with an explicit sediment compartment and sediment–water exchange can simulate. This is consequential now precisely because Michigan lowered its fish PFOS "Do Not Eat" threshold from 300 to 50 ppb, sharply expanding the set of advisory waterbodies. We emphasize that the sediment-legacy mechanism is, at present, an inference drawn by Endicott et al. (2025) from converging lines of evidence rather than a closed mass balance; our contribution is to provide the dynamic, sediment-coupled framework positioned to test and quantify it, not to assert it as already proven.

This study is the direct follow-up to our prior distributed PFAS model of the same watershed (Rafiei & Nejadhashemi 2023), and two distinctions are load-bearing. First, that earlier work was a SWAT-MODFLOW-RT3D model resolving roughly 189 reaches, whereas the present work is a SWAT+ port resolving 3,119 channels. Second, the earlier source-apportionment effort was conducted *before* regulatory source control, whereas the present model targets the *post-control regime* in which the water-column drop and fish-tissue plateau have decoupled. The PFAS soil-equilibrium physics of the present model is ported from—not reinvented relative to—that prior work.

### 1.4 Objectives and scope

We present a process-based PFAS surface-water fate-and-transport model of the Huron River watershed (HUC8 04100013, southeastern Michigan), generated in SWAT+ by the SWATGenX platform from NHDPlus HR hydrography, 1/3 arc-second 3DEP elevation, gSSURGO soils, and gridded land-use and climate forcings. The model resolves the network into 3,119 channels and 72,475 hydrologic response units (HRUs)—approximately an order-of-magnitude increase in stream-network resolution over the prior distributed model of the same watershed (Rafiei & Nejadhashemi 2023) [the precise ~16.5× ratio is VERIFY-gated on confirming the 189-reach denominator in the prior paper]. This resolution gain resides in network geometry and observation assignment, both supplied at high resolution by NHDPlus HR, and not in the resolution of meteorological forcing or PFAS source inventories; we state this proactively to avoid overclaiming. Its decisive methodological consequence is that each PFAS field observation can be assigned to its hydrologically correct channel by closest drainage area rather than to a coarse aggregated subbasin, reducing the representativeness error that grows as the ratio of observed to modeled upstream drainage area departs from unity (Farmer 2018). Because PFOS partitions strongly to organic carbon and sediment, it belongs to the resolution-sensitive constituent class for which discretization affects load estimates in a biased rather than merely noisy manner (Jha et al. 2004; FitzHugh & Mackay 2000), so the finer network is a methodological advance rather than cosmetic refinement. We scope this first version of the engine to PFOS only; the 4 ng/L MCL is PFOA/PFOS-specific, and we make no class-wide claim from PFOS results. Equifinality is *managed*—through global Morris sensitivity screening and physically grouped, class-based parameters rather than independent per-reach parameters—not eliminated.

The contribution of this work is threefold. We port a soil three-phase PFAS equilibrium formulation from a prior SWAT-MODFLOW-RT3D model onto the modern SWAT+ constituent host, re-implemented in double precision and validated against a high-precision reference, and we couple it to a reach-resolved land-phase and in-stream transport framework. We introduce a high-resolution observation-to-channel assignment that, for the first time in this watershed, places each PFAS measurement on its hydrologically correct reach by closest drainage area, sharpening both calibration targets and source attribution. And we position the model against the watershed's defining post-control observation—the water-column drop with a fish-tissue plateau—as a demanding dynamic test of water–sediment partitioning that fitting a single concentration series could not provide. Against machine-learning and monitoring alternatives, the contribution is one of capability rather than goodness-of-fit: the process model is the causal and forecasting tier those tools structurally cannot reach, complementary to their occurrence-triage strength, and it is timely because the post-2024 landscape of enforceable 4.0 ng/L MCLs and Michigan's 300→50 ppb fish-tissue threshold shifts the management question from detection toward forecasting and reach-scale intervention design.

## 2. Study Area

### 2.1 Physiography and setting

The Huron River watershed (HUC8 04100013) drains approximately 2,400 km² [VERIFY] of southeastern Michigan, rising in the morainal uplands of Oakland and Livingston counties and flowing roughly 200 km [VERIFY] southeast through Washtenaw and Wayne counties before discharging to Lake Erie at the western end of the lake near Rockwood. The watershed spans a pronounced land-use gradient that makes it a representative test bed for PFAS source-and-transport processes: glacially derived forest, wetland, and lake-and-kettle terrain in the headwaters; an intensively cultivated and pastured agricultural midsection; and a heavily urbanized lower corridor encompassing the cities of Wixom and Milford in the upper watershed and Ann Arbor and Ypsilanti in the lower watershed. The mainstem is impounded at multiple points, most consequentially at Kent Lake (Kensington Metropark), a run-of-river reservoir whose long residence time concentrates depositional sediment and which figures centrally in the watershed's contaminant history (Section 2.3). This physiographic diversity—forest-to-urban land cover, a chain of impoundments, and a well-instrumented mainstem—lets a single watershed exercise the full set of PFAS land-phase pathways (forest background, agricultural and biosolids-associated loading, urban and industrial point sources) within one hydrologically coherent domain.

### 2.2 Why the Huron is a canonical PFAS test watershed

Three features make the Huron River an unusually decisive watershed for process-based PFAS modeling. First, it carries a well-documented industrial PFAS source with a dated, near-step-change control history (Section 2.3), which supplies a natural before/after experiment that a fate-and-transport model can be tested against. Second, it supports a regulatorily binding human-exposure pathway on two fronts—drinking water and fish consumption—so model predictions map directly onto enforceable thresholds rather than onto purely diagnostic endpoints. The city of Ann Arbor draws roughly 85% of its municipal drinking-water supply from a Huron River surface-water intake at Barton Pond, and in August 2023 the utility adopted a PFAS Action Plan setting self-imposed finished-water targets of <8 ng/L for PFOS+PFOA and <50 ng/L total PFAS (Ann Arbor 2023); a Huron-sourced PFAS concentration is therefore a direct input to a regulated public-water system serving more than 120,000 people [VERIFY population]. Third, the watershed is among the most intensively monitored PFAS systems in the Great Lakes basin, with a multi-year ambient surface-water record maintained by the Michigan Department of Environment, Great Lakes, and Energy (EGLE) and a parallel fish-tissue monitoring program (Section 2.4). The convergence of a dated source perturbation, two enforceable exposure endpoints, and a dense observational record is what allows the watershed to function as a calibration-and-validation test bed rather than merely a case study.

### 2.3 Source history and the post-control sediment-legacy regime

The dominant historical PFAS source to the upper Huron is the Tribar Manufacturing chrome-plating facility in Wixom, whose process wastewater reached the river via the Wixom municipal sewer, the Wixom Wastewater Treatment Plant, and Norton Creek, entering the mainstem roughly 8 km (≈5 mi) above Kent Lake. Installation of carbon treatment at the source in 2018 produced a near-complete water-column response: PFOS in Norton Creek fell from approximately 5,600 to 12.2 ng/L (−99.8%), and in the Huron mainstem below Norton Creek from approximately 1,400 to 6.1 ng/L by August 2020 (primary station values [VERIFY against EGLE Water Resources Division investigation reports]). The source itself, however, was never fully eliminated: following the facility's closure and relocation to Howell (2024–2025 [VERIFY exact date]), Michigan's Attorney General filed a June 2025 civil action alleging that the shuttered Wixom sites continued to discharge PFOS-bearing stormwater, with one outfall reported at 220,000 ng/L (allegation; value and sampling location [VERIFY against the complaint]). The watershed's PFAS source forcing is thus bimodal in time—a large, well-dated point-source step-down in 2018, followed by a residual diffuse stormwater pathway in the post-closure period—a structure the calibration window must accommodate.

The scientifically pivotal observation emerges from this post-control regime. Despite the ~99% water-column decline, fish-tissue PFOS in the Huron system—Kent Lake in particular—has plateaued above the consumption-advisory threshold rather than tracking the water-column recovery (Endicott et al. 2025). This divergence has been attributed to contaminated bed sediment acting as a secondary, internal source that continues to supply PFOS to the food web after external loading was curtailed. The regulatory weight of this plateau increased sharply in June 2025, when the Michigan Department of Health and Human Services lowered the PFOS Do-Not-Eat fish-tissue trigger from 300 to 50 ppb (MDHHS 2025), making the binding constraint on this system fish tissue rather than water-column concentration. A dynamic, sediment-coupled, reach-resolved process model is the natural instrument for mechanistically testing whether sediment legacy alone can reproduce this observed plateau—the central motivation developed in Section 1.

### 2.4 Available PFAS observations

Ambient PFAS surface-water data for the watershed are drawn principally from the EGLE surface-water monitoring program, which maintains per-station aqueous PFAS concentrations (reported in ng/L) accessible through its PFAS Surface Water data service, supplemented by USEPA and Water Quality Portal (WQP) records. These ambient stations constitute the in-stream calibration spine of the present work and are assigned to their hydrologically correct channel reaches by the drainage-area-aware procedure described in Section 3.4; a test sub-watershed demonstration snapped 58 EGLE stations to the SWAT+ network at a median planar distance of 3.9 m, with the full HUC8 station set being larger. Fish-tissue PFOS observations—from the EGLE Fish Contaminant Monitoring Program (including Huron fillet sampling ongoing as of early 2026) and from the Kent Lake tissue-and-sediment dataset underlying Endicott et al. (2025)—provide the independent, regulation-aligned validation target against the 50 ppb advisory threshold. We note that the watershed's PFAS signature is multi-component (recent work reports PFHxA as dominant in the water column and PFOS plus emerging fluorinated compounds in sediment); the present model is restricted to PFOS in this first version (Section 1.4), and the fish-tissue and emerging-compound observations are reserved for validation and future multi-congener extension rather than v1 calibration.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Model generation and resolution

#### Automated model generation

The watershed model was generated by the SWATGenX platform, an automated pipeline that assembles a fully parameterized SWAT+ model for any drainage in the conterminous United States directly from national geospatial archives, with no manual delineation or hand-editing of inputs. For the Huron River watershed (HUC8 04100013) the pipeline ingested five primary data sources: the National Hydrography Dataset Plus High Resolution (NHDPlus HR) for the stream network and catchment topology; the 1/3 arc-second (~10 m) 3D Elevation Program (3DEP) digital elevation model (DEM) for terrain and flow-direction conditioning; the gridded Soil Survey Geographic Database (gSSURGO) for soil horizons and their physical and chemical properties; the National Land Cover Database (NLCD) together with the USDA Cropland Data Layer (CDL) for land use and management, rasterized at 250 m; and Parameter-elevation Regressions on Independent Slopes Model (PRISM) fields for daily precipitation and temperature forcing. The hydrography drives the channel network, the conditioned 3DEP DEM supplies slopes and flow routing, and the gSSURGO–NLCD/CDL intersection defines the hydrologic response units (HRUs) that carry the land-phase mass balance.

The resulting model discretizes the Huron watershed into **72,475 HRUs**, **3,119 stream reaches (channels)**, approximately **3,430 routing units**, and roughly **90 reservoirs**. Each HRU is a unique land use–soil–slope combination within a routing unit and is the elementary control volume on which the land-phase water, sediment, and PFAS balances are solved; each channel is an explicit NHDPlus HR flowline carrying the in-stream balance. The serial engine requires approximately **2.85 GB** peak memory and roughly **13 minutes per simulated year**, placing multi-decadal, ensemble-based calibration within reach of a parallelized, cloud-dispatched workflow (Section 3.5). Because every input is a national-coverage archive and every processing step is scripted, the same generation procedure transfers to any HUC8 in the conterminous United States without re-tooling—a reproducibility property we return to in the Discussion rather than claim as a core scientific advance.

#### Why reach-scale resolution is a methodological advance, not a cosmetic refinement

This discretization represents an approximately **16.5× increase in stream-network resolution** over the authors' prior distributed model of the same watershed (~189 reaches; Rafiei & Nejadhashemi 2023) [VERIFY the 189-reach denominator against the 2023 paper's reported discretization before printing the 16.5× ratio; until verified, state "approximately an order of magnitude"]. The gain is consequential for PFOS transport for three reasons, each anchored in the established discretization-sensitivity and drainage-area-transfer literature; we state proactively that the advance is in *network geometry and observation assignment*, not in the resolution of meteorological forcing or in the dimensionality of the parameter vector.

First, **PFOS belongs to the resolution-sensitive constituent class.** Watershed-subdivision studies consistently find that simulated streamflow is largely insensitive to subbasin count, whereas sediment- and particulate-associated constituents are strongly sensitive: Jha et al. (2004) show that stabilizing sediment loads requires subbasin drainage areas below roughly 3% of total watershed area (about 2% for nitrate and 5% for phosphorus), and FitzHugh and Mackay (2000) show that the sediment response to coarsening is systematically biased rather than merely noisy, propagating through a nonlinear source-area relationship. Because PFOS partitions strongly to organic carbon and sediment (Section 3.2), it is governed by the same physics that places sediment in the resolution-sensitive class; the applicable discretization standard is therefore the ~2–5% sediment/nutrient threshold, which a 189-reach delineation cannot satisfy and a 3,119-reach delineation can.

Second, **the resolution gain is inherited from the input hydrography and restores headwater reaches that coarser networks omit.** NHDPlus HR is built on 1:24,000-scale (or finer) hydrography conditioned to the 1/3 arc-second 3DEP DEM, against the 1:100,000-scale basis of the medium-resolution NHDPlus V2 (Moore et al. 2019). Coarser networks do not merely lump headwaters; they delete them—and first-order streams are both the most abundant channel type and disproportionate sites of in-stream processing owing to their high benthic-area-to-volume ratios (Alexander et al. 2007). The finer network reinstates these reaches as explicit modeling units, so the added resolution is data-supported rather than an arbitrary modeling choice.

Third, and most central to this study, the fine network underpins a **closest-drainage-area observation-to-channel assignment** (detailed in Section 3.4). Every PFAS field measurement is collected at a real upstream drainage area; assigning it to a model channel introduces a representativeness error that scales with the mismatch between the sample's true contributing area and that of the assigned reach. Drainage-area-ratio (DAR) transfer theory establishes that such error grows monotonically as the area ratio departs from unity, with reliable transfer commonly bounded near a 0.5–1.5 ratio (Asquith et al. 2006), and Farmer (2018) shows explicitly that higher network resolution reduces this representativeness error. At 189 reaches an observation is forced onto a coarse aggregate that integrates tributaries it never sampled; at 3,119 reaches the nearest channel by drainage area is a far tighter match, drawing the assignment ratio toward unity so that each simulated reach concentration is evaluated against an observation that genuinely represents that reach. Moreover, where PFOS loads and the sediment-legacy inventory implicated at Kent Lake are spatially concentrated—the documented norm for diffuse loading, in which roughly 80% of load originates from roughly 20% of area (Djodjic & Markensten 2018)—the finer network resolves contributing reaches that a coarse model would dilution-bias into invisibility. We caution, however, that the DAR validity bands were derived for transferring *streamflow* between hydrologically similar catchments, whereas we use closest drainage area as a station-to-reach *snapping* criterion; we invoke DAR theory only as the conceptual scale of acceptable area mismatch and the principle that representativeness error grows with departure from unity, not as a numerically transferable validity band.

#### Costs and limits, stated explicitly

A finer network carries genuine costs, which we manage rather than dismiss. The most common objection—over-parameterization and the equifinality it invites—is substantially blunted by the SWAT+ parameter structure: transport parameters are assigned by class (land use, soil, and source type), not independently per reach, so moving from 189 to 3,119 reaches raises the spatial resolution of *prediction* far faster than the dimension of the *calibration vector*. We further constrain calibration to physically grouped parameters and apply global Morris sensitivity screening to fix non-influential parameters before fitting (Section 3.5); equifinality is thereby *managed*, not eliminated. A second limit is forcing-resolution mismatch: we claim no sub-reach certainty in meteorology or in the PFAS source inventory, and we treat those coarser inputs as the limiting resolution for source-attribution confidence. Third, we are not over-resolving past numerical convergence—the Jha et al. (2004) thresholds place the 189-reach model on the non-converged side and the present model on the converged side of the sediment/nutrient stability criterion. Finally, the added compute cost is real but tractable through parallelization and cloud-dispatched calibration; it is an engineering cost, justified by the reach-scale decisions (the 4 ng/L MCL, the 50 ppb fish advisory) the model is built to inform, not a scientific barrier. To convert the central assignment argument from a mechanistic claim into a measured one specific to the Huron PFAS network, we plan a diagnostic figure (Fig. R2) comparing, for the actual ambient stations, the histogram of |log(DAR)| = |log(A_station / A_assigned reach)| under the 189-reach and 3,119-reach networks, together with the fraction of stations falling inside the 0.5–1.5 area-ratio band under each.

### 3.2 PFAS process model and engine

The PFAS module reproduces the soil-column geochemistry of the SWAT-MODFLOW-RT3D formulation of Rafiei & Nejadhashemi (2023), re-implemented natively on the SWAT+ pesticide-constituent host so that PFOS is advected and partitioned within the modern engine's HRU and routing architecture. As in the source model, the constituent is treated as a single soluble–sorbable species (PFOS only in v1) distributed across three coexisting phases within each soil layer: a dissolved aqueous phase, a solid-sorbed phase, and an adsorbed film at the air–water interface. The latter phase is the distinguishing feature of PFAS relative to conventional hydrophobic-organic constituents: as surfactants, perfluoroalkyl acids accumulate at air–water menisci in the unsaturated zone, a retention mechanism that is negligible for most pesticides but first-order for PFOS (Brusseau 2018; Li et al. 2019).

**Governing soil equilibrium.** For each soil layer we impose local equilibrium among the three phases and solve for the aqueous concentration $C_w$ (mass per pore-water volume) that conserves the layer's total PFOS mass $M_{tot}$. Writing $\theta$ for volumetric water content, $\rho_b$ for bulk density, and $A_{aw}$ for the specific air–water interfacial area per unit bulk volume, the per-layer mass-balance residual is

$$
F(C_w) \;=\; \underbrace{\theta\,C_w}_{\text{aqueous}} \;+\; \underbrace{\rho_b\,k_f\,C_w^{\,n}}_{\text{Freundlich solid}} \;+\; \underbrace{A_{aw}\,\frac{\Gamma_{max}\,K_L\,C_w}{1+K_L\,C_w}}_{\text{air–water Langmuir}} \;-\; M_{tot} \;=\; 0,
$$

where the solid phase follows a Freundlich isotherm with coefficient $k_f$ and exponent $n$, and the air–water interfacial film follows a Langmuir isotherm with maximum surface excess $\Gamma_{max}$ (nmol m$^{-2}$) and affinity $K_L$ (L nmol$^{-1}$). The air–water interfacial area $A_{aw}$ is derived per layer from soil moisture and the median grain diameter $d_{50}$, consistent with the source-model parameterization. Because $F$ superposes a linear aqueous term, a power-law solid term ($0<n<1$), and a saturating Langmuir term, it is monotonically increasing in $C_w$ on the physical domain $C_w>0$ and admits a unique positive root; no closed-form inverse exists.

**Root-finding solver.** We solve $F(C_w)=0$ per layer per time step with a three-stage strategy. The root is first **bracketed** by expanding an interval $[C_{lo}, C_{hi}]$ until $F$ changes sign, exploiting monotonicity to guarantee a bracket that contains the unique root. **Bisection** then contracts the bracket to a robust neighborhood of the root, after which an **Improved-Halley** iteration—a third-order method using $F$, $F'$, and $F''$ (all available analytically from the isotherm terms)—converges to machine tolerance in few iterations while remaining bracket-safeguarded against overshoot. We re-implemented the entire solver in IEEE double precision; the original formulation used `real(16)` quadruple precision, which we found unnecessary. Across 29,160 test cases spanning the full parameter envelope, the double-precision solver reproduced a 40-digit arbitrary-precision reference root to a worst-case relative error of $1.7\times10^{-7}$, well within the tolerance required for mass-conservative transport and at a fraction of the quad-precision cost.

**Land-phase loss pathways.** Once the layer equilibrium is resolved, the equilibrium aqueous concentration $C_w$ drives the partition of PFOS among the land-phase export fluxes generated by the SWAT+ hydrologic engine. Dissolved PFOS leaves the HRU with (i) **surface runoff**, (ii) **lateral subsurface flow**, and (iii) **percolation/leaching** to the layer below and ultimately to the shallow aquifer, each flux carrying the layer's aqueous concentration scaled by the corresponding water volume. The sorbed phase is mobilized as a **sediment-bound** export, computed as the Freundlich solid-phase concentration multiplied by the HRU sediment yield and a sediment **enrichment ratio** that accounts for the preferential erosion of fine, organic-rich particles to which PFOS preferentially partitions. This four-pathway partition (runoff / lateral / leaching / sediment-bound) reproduces the land-phase export structure of the source model while inheriting the SWAT+ water and sediment balances.

**In-stream transport.** Consistent with the source model, the soil three-phase equilibrium (Freundlich solid plus air–water Langmuir) is applied **only in the soil column**. Once PFOS reaches the channel network it is transported using a **linear organic-carbon partition (linear-$K_{oc}$)** description, in which dissolved and particulate-sorbed in-stream PFOS are related by a constant $K_{oc}$ rather than by the nonlinear Freundlich/Langmuir isotherms. This deliberately matches the in-stream representation of Rafiei & Nejadhashemi (2023) and reflects the lower solid-to-water ratios and shorter contact times of the water column relative to the soil profile. Reach-resolved in-stream PFOS concentrations are a forthcoming result, pending completion of the in-stream module and flow/PFAS calibration; we report the in-stream formulation here as the transport structure that calibration will exercise, not as a calibrated output.

**SWAT+ implementation and concurrency.** The PFAS constituent is carried on the SWAT+ pesticide-constituent host, which already provides per-HRU, per-layer constituent storage and the land-to-channel delivery hooks; the soil equilibrium solver and the linear-$K_{oc}$ in-stream term are inserted into this host without altering the engine's hydrology. The land phase is **HRU-parallel**—each HRU's soil equilibrium and export partition are independent and computed concurrently—while channel **routing is executed serially** in hydrologic order. Because every land-phase write targets a distinct HRU and the routing pass reads HRU exports only after the parallel land phase completes, the scheme is byte-identical to serial execution and free of read–write races on the in-stream water-quality state, so reach concentrations are reproducible irrespective of thread count.

**Mass-balance verification.** We verified land-phase conservation by closing the per-time-step PFOS budget—initial layer storage plus inputs, minus the runoff, lateral, leaching, and sediment-bound exports, minus residual storage—over the test model. The closure residual is $\sim 1\times10^{-5}$ in relative terms, confirming that the layer equilibrium solver and the export partition together conserve constituent mass to within numerical tolerance and do not introduce spurious sources or sinks. Parameter values used to initialize the equilibrium—$k_f$, $n$, $d_{50}$, $K_L$, $\Gamma_{max}$, and the PFOS physicochemical constants—are given in Section 3.3.

### 3.3 Soil-PFAS initialization

The soil compartment is the principal reservoir and long-term source term for PFOS in this system, so its initialization governs both the magnitude and the spatial structure of simulated loading. We initialize two distinct classes of quantity at the resolution of each HRU soil layer: (i) the sorption parameters that control three-phase partitioning—the Freundlich capacity coefficient $k_f$, the Freundlich exponent $n$, and the median grain diameter $d_{50}$—which are derived deterministically from soil properties already carried in the model; and (ii) the initial solid-phase PFOS concentration, which is assigned as a literature-anchored *range* by land use and source proximity and then handed to calibration. We state at the outset which steps are automated from national data and which are anchored to literature values, because the two carry different epistemic weight: the sorption parameters are physically constrained by measured soil texture and organic carbon, whereas the initial concentrations are bounded estimates that calibration is expected to refine within their assigned envelopes.

#### Sorption parameters from gSSURGO

PFOS solid–aqueous partitioning is represented by the Freundlich isotherm $C_s = k_f\,C_w^{\,n}$ introduced in Section 3.2, consistent with the source model (Rafiei & Nejadhashemi 2023). The pair ($k_f$, $n$) and the grain diameter $d_{50}$ are assigned by analogy matching: for each HRU soil layer we extract organic-carbon content and texture (clay/sand fractions) from gSSURGO—properties the SWATGenX pipeline already ingests nationally for every layer—and classify the layer to whichever of six reference soils, S1–S6, most closely matches in organic carbon and texture by nearest-neighbor distance. The reference soils and their isotherm parameters are taken from the controlled PFOS sorption measurements of Li et al. (2019), spanning $k_f$ ≈ 126–450, $n$ ≈ 0.33–0.53, and $d_{50}$ ≈ 0.04–0.10 mm across the set [VERIFY the per-soil S1–S6 ($k_f$, $n$, $d_{50}$) values against Li et al. (2019); the numeric table was not reproduced in the source-model supplement and must be transcribed from the primary]. Because organic carbon is the dominant control on PFOS solid-phase affinity, this match propagates the spatial texture and organic-carbon heterogeneity already resolved by gSSURGO into a spatially varying sorption field at no additional data cost. This is the most fully automated element of the initialization: it requires no PFAS-specific input layer, only the soil survey that underlies every SWATGenX model. In calibration the $k_f$ field is allowed to vary within a bounded multiplier of its analogy-derived value (Section 3.5), preserving the gSSURGO-imposed spatial pattern while admitting a global scaling adjustment.

#### Initial solid-phase PFOS by land use and source overlay

Initial PFOS concentrations cannot be drawn from a queryable national measured-soil layer, because none exists at the spatial completeness this model requires; we therefore adopt the tiered, range-based scheme of the source model (Rafiei & Nejadhashemi 2023), generalized to national data sources. Every HRU first receives a land-use background range, classified from NLCD/CDL: forest and natural cover 0.005–0.01 µg/kg, agricultural and pasture 0.2–8 µg/kg, and developed/urban 0.2–30 µg/kg. These bounds reflect the order-of-magnitude separation between remote and developed soils reported in large soil-PFAS compilations (Brusseau et al. 2020) and are consistent with the source model.

Onto this background we apply additive national source overlays for known and presumptive contaminated sites, identified by spatial intersection rather than distance decay—gradients in the simulated field are allowed to emerge from transport rather than being imposed at initialization. HRUs intersecting PFAS-handling facilities from the U.S. EPA Toxics Release Inventory and Facility Registry Service (TRI/FRS), FAA Part 139–certificated airports (a proxy for aqueous film-forming foam, AFFF, fire-training activity), or Department of Defense AFFF installations are assigned an elevated source-zone range of 100–4,000 µg/kg, with the elevated concentration confined to a clamped source-pad footprint rather than the full facility buffer. This reproduces the source model's contaminated-site treatment while substituting national, programmatically queryable federal layers (TRI/FRS via EPA Envirofacts, the FAA Part 139 list, and DoD installation inventories) for the state-specific facility data of the original Huron application. Where state data are richer than the federal layers—as for Michigan—they may supplement, but are not required by, the national pipeline.

All concentrations are emitted as *ranges*, not point values, and these ranges constitute prior bounds handed to calibration. We make this explicit because it bears directly on the claim discipline of the work: the initialization fixes the spatial *structure* of the source field (which HRUs are background or source-zone, and how sorption varies with soil) from data, while leaving the operative *magnitudes* within each class to be identified by the optimizer. This is a managed treatment of equifinality, not an elimination of it; the ranges are wide by design, and the burden of constraining them falls on calibration against in-stream observations. For the PFOS constituent in v1, the physicochemical constants are molecular weight 0.50013 kg mol$^{-1}$, aqueous solubility 680 mg L$^{-1}$, Langmuir affinity $K_L = 0.137$ L nmol$^{-1}$ [VERIFY against the source paper], and maximum surface excess $\Gamma_{max} = 2500$ nmol m$^{-2}$ [VERIFY].

### 3.4 Observation-to-channel assignment

Testing simulated PFOS against ambient observations requires that each monitoring record be associated with the channel reach it actually represents. In a coarsely discretized model this association is ambiguous—an observation may fall within an aggregated subbasin that lumps a contaminated tributary with unaffected drainage—and the resulting representativeness error is conflated with model error during calibration. The fine network resolved here (3,119 channels) reduces that ambiguity by providing reach units close to the scale of the observations themselves.

Ambient PFAS surface-water stations are drawn from the Michigan EGLE surface-water monitoring program and the USEPA Water Quality Portal (WQP). Each station is snapped to the nearest SWAT+ channel reach by planar distance in the CONUS Albers equal-area projection (EPSG:5070), with the candidate reach selected to be consistent in drainage area—the assignment is closest-drainage-area-aware rather than purely geometric, so a station is matched to a reach of comparable contributing area rather than to a physically nearer but hydrologically inconsistent channel. The drainage-area criterion is motivated conceptually by drainage-area-ratio reasoning, under which streamflow and load scale approximately with contributing area; as in Section 3.1, we use DAR as a conceptual scale for representativeness, not as a numerically transferable validity band. On a test sub-watershed, 58 EGLE stations were assigned by this procedure with a median snap distance of 3.9 m, indicating that at this network resolution the great majority of stations fall essentially on a resolved channel rather than requiring substantial displacement; the full HUC8 station set is larger, and its assignment statistics are reported with the results. The reach-resolved network and this assignment procedure together establish the spatial correspondence on which the in-stream calibration of Section 3.5 depends.

### 3.5 Calibration and validation design

Because in-stream PFOS transport is conditioned on the simulated water balance, calibration proceeds in two sequential stages—flow first, then PFAS parameters—so that hydrologic forcing is constrained before solute-transport parameters are fit, and PFAS-parameter error is not aliased onto flow error.

**Flow calibration.** Streamflow is calibrated against daily discharge at the U.S. Geological Survey gauges within and immediately downstream of HUC8 04100013, using the multi-memory particle-swarm optimizer (MMPSO) implemented on the SWATGenX platform, with single-population PSO retained as a fallback. Optimization uses an ensemble of approximately 24 particles [VERIFY final swarm size] over a Latin-hypercube-seeded initial design. Prior to optimization, a Morris elementary-effects screening (Morris 1991) ranks the candidate flow parameters—curve number, available water capacity, baseflow recession (the alpha and groundwater-delay terms), lateral-flow travel time, and channel hydraulic conductivity and roughness, among others—so that non-influential parameters are fixed at default and only the sensitive subset enters the calibration vector; this is the recognized first-line control on equifinality (Beven & Binley 1992) and keeps the dimension of the search tractable despite the fine discretization. Goodness of fit is assessed with the Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE) and the Kling–Gupta efficiency (KGE) at daily and monthly aggregation, with percent bias (PBIAS) reported to constrain volumetric error. Predictive uncertainty is summarized by the P-factor (the fraction of observations bracketed by the 95% prediction band) and the R-factor (the mean band width relative to the observed standard deviation), following the SWAT-CUP convention (Abbaspour et al. 2015); a P-factor near or above the conventional 0.7 acceptability guideline with a contained R-factor is the target. A warm-up period precedes the evaluation window, and the record is partitioned into calibration and validation sub-periods so that performance is reported on data withheld from fitting.

**PFAS-parameter calibration.** With the water balance fixed, PFOS transport parameters are calibrated against Michigan EGLE and USEPA/WQP in-stream PFOS observations assigned to their hydrologically correct channels by the closest-drainage-area procedure of Section 3.4. The calibration does not treat soil concentrations as free scalars; instead it samples within the physically bounded ranges already emitted by the initialization—the land-use-conditioned initial soil PFOS bands (forest 0.005–0.01, agricultural/pasture 0.2–8, urban 0.2–30 µg/kg) and the national-source-overlay band (EPA TRI/FRS, FAA Part 139, DoD AFFF sites, 100–4,000 µg/kg)—so that adjustment remains within source-attributable, literature-consistent limits rather than drifting to arbitrary best-fit values. The Freundlich solid-phase capacity $k_f$, mapped per layer from the six reference soils (S1–S6), is allowed to vary within ±30% of its assigned value to absorb local organic-carbon and texture mismatch while preserving the soil-class ordering; the Freundlich exponent $n$ and the in-stream linear-$K_{oc}$ partitioning coefficient are treated as the additional adjustable transport parameters, again screened by Morris before fitting and grouped by class (land use, soil, source type) rather than assigned independently per reach, which holds the calibration-vector dimension nearly constant as reach count rises. Agreement between simulated and observed in-stream PFOS is evaluated at the assigned reaches using NSE/KGE on the concentration series where the observational density supports it, supplemented by paired observed-versus-simulated error statistics (PBIAS, RMSE) where records are sparse, with the same P-factor/R-factor uncertainty reporting.

**Dynamic validation.** Beyond static fit, the calibrated model is confronted with the watershed's post-source-control trajectory: the ~99% water-column PFOS decline following upstream control, and the contrasting fish-tissue plateau attributed to sediment legacy (Endicott et al. 2025). Reproducing the water-column recovery while remaining consistent with a persistent sediment-bound reservoir is a demanding test of the model's water–sediment partitioning that fitting a single concentration series could not provide. Throughout, equifinality is treated as managed—through Morris screening, class-based parameter grouping, and explicit uncertainty bands—rather than eliminated.

## 5. Discussion

The capabilities this model is built to supply follow directly from its process structure and its reach resolution. By routing PFOS separately through surface runoff, lateral flow, leaching, and sediment-bound pathways and resolving each onto its hydrologically correct channel, the model is positioned to perform the **source attribution** that CERCLA allocation requires—disaggregating an observed in-stream concentration into pathway- and source-zone contributions that a grab sample integrates and a statistical occurrence model cannot recover. The same structure yields a mass-conserving concentration at every one of the 3,119 reaches, supplying **prediction in ungauged reaches** where monitoring is structurally absent, and it permits **counterfactual evaluation** of the interventions that are the regulator's actual levers—upstream source control, biosolids management, sediment remediation, or a wasteload allocation—because an intervention is represented as a change to a pathway rather than to an outcome.

The watershed's defining observation is where these capabilities converge. The decoupling of a ~99% water-column decline from a fish-tissue plateau above advisory is the signature of a slow-release internal sediment reservoir, and it is precisely the kind of non-steady behavior that equilibrium or source-inventory accounting cannot reproduce. We are careful to frame the sediment-legacy mechanism as the observation the model is positioned to *test and quantify*—via the Fig. R7 scenario contrasting recovery trajectories with and without a sediment internal source—rather than as a closed mass balance the model has already proven; the mechanism remains, at present, an inference drawn by Endicott et al. (2025) from converging lines of evidence. Turning that inference into a quantified, time-resolved contribution, and into a forecast of how long the sediment inventory will sustain exceedance, is the contribution the dynamic framework is built to make.

Two limits bound these claims. First, the engine is **PFOS-only in v1**: the 4 ng/L MCL is PFOA/PFOS-specific, the watershed's signature is multi-component, and we make no class-wide claim from PFOS results; multi-congener extension is reserved for future work. Second, the resolution advance is in network geometry and observation assignment, not in the resolution of meteorological forcing or PFAS source inventories, and in-stream process fidelity—residence time, sorption, resuspension kinetics—is a structural capability of the finer network rather than a demonstrated accuracy gain, which reach-scale process data would be required to prove. Finally, the automated NHDPlus HR→SWAT+ pipeline transfers to any HUC8 in the conterminous United States without re-tooling; we present this **national scalability as reproducibility and transferability** rather than as a core scientific advance, since high-resolution SWAT+ generation is increasingly common, but it is what makes the framework deployable at the scale the regulatory landscape now demands.

---

## 6. Conclusions

We have presented the generation, PFAS process formulation, soil-PFAS initialization, and observation-to-channel assignment of a process-based, reach-resolved PFOS surface-water fate-and-transport model of the Huron River watershed, built in SWAT+ by the SWATGenX platform from national geospatial archives. The capability contribution is to supply the causal and forecasting tier—source attribution, ungauged-reach prediction, and counterfactual intervention design—that monitoring and statistical occurrence models structurally cannot reach. The methodological advance is the combination of an approximately order-of-magnitude increase in stream-network resolution with a closest-drainage-area observation-to-channel assignment that, for the first time in this watershed, evaluates each PFAS measurement against the reach it genuinely represents. The work is timely because the post-2024 landscape of enforceable 4.0 ng/L PFOA/PFOS MCLs, the CERCLA designation, Michigan's Rule 57 in-stream criteria, and the lowered 50 ppb fish-tissue threshold together shift the management question from detection toward forecasting and reach-scale intervention design—and the model's native output is the quantity these regulations constrain. Flow and PFAS calibration, the diagnostic quantification of the assignment advantage, and the sediment-legacy recovery forecast are forthcoming.
