## 2. Study Area

### 2.1 Physiography and setting

The Huron River watershed (HUC8 04100013) drains approximately 2,400 km² [VERIFY] of southeastern Michigan, rising in the morainal uplands of Oakland and Livingston counties and flowing roughly 200 km southeast through Washtenaw and Wayne counties before discharging to Lake Erie at the western end of the lake near Rockwood. The watershed spans a pronounced land-use gradient that makes it a representative test bed for PFAS source-and-transport processes: glacially derived forest, wetland, and lake-and-kettle terrain in the headwaters; an intensively cultivated and pastured agricultural midsection; and a heavily urbanized lower corridor encompassing the cities of Wixom and Milford in the upper watershed and Ann Arbor and Ypsilanti in the lower watershed. The mainstem is impounded at multiple points, most consequentially at Kent Lake (Kensington Metropark), a run-of-river reservoir whose long residence time concentrates depositional sediment and which figures centrally in the watershed's contaminant history (Section 2.3). This physiographic diversity—forest-to-urban land cover, a chain of impoundments, and a well-instrumented mainstem—lets a single watershed exercise the full set of PFAS land-phase pathways (forest background, agricultural and biosolids-associated loading, urban and industrial point sources) within one hydrologically coherent domain.

### 2.2 Why the Huron is a canonical PFAS test watershed

Three features make the Huron River an unusually decisive watershed for process-based PFAS modeling. First, it carries a well-documented industrial PFAS source with a dated, near-step-change control history (Section 2.3), which supplies a natural before/after experiment that a fate-and-transport model can be tested against. Second, it supports a regulatorily binding human-exposure pathway on two fronts—drinking water and fish consumption—so model predictions map directly onto enforceable thresholds rather than onto purely diagnostic endpoints. The city of Ann Arbor draws roughly 85% of its municipal drinking-water supply from a Huron River surface-water intake at Barton Pond, and in August 2023 the utility adopted a PFAS Action Plan setting self-imposed finished-water targets of <8 ng/L for PFOS+PFOA and <50 ng/L total PFAS (Ann Arbor 2023); a Huron-sourced PFAS concentration is therefore a direct input to a regulated public-water system serving >120,000 people [VERIFY population]. Third, the watershed is among the most intensively monitored PFAS systems in the Great Lakes basin, with a multi-year ambient surface-water record maintained by the Michigan Department of Environment, Great Lakes, and Energy (EGLE) and a parallel fish-tissue monitoring program (Section 2.4). The convergence of a dated source perturbation, two enforceable exposure endpoints, and a dense observational record is what allows the watershed to function as a calibration-and-validation test bed rather than merely a case study.

### 2.3 Source history and the post-control sediment-legacy regime

The dominant historical PFAS source to the upper Huron is the Tribar Manufacturing chrome-plating facility in Wixom, whose process wastewater reached the river via the Wixom municipal sewer, the Wixom Wastewater Treatment Plant, and Norton Creek, entering the mainstem roughly 8 km (≈5 mi) above Kent Lake. Installation of carbon treatment at the source in 2018 produced a near-complete water-column response: PFOS in Norton Creek fell from approximately 5,600 to 12.2 ng/L (−99.8%), and in the Huron mainstem below Norton Creek from approximately 1,400 to 6.1 ng/L by August 2020 (EGLE, as synthesized in the project dossier; primary station values [VERIFY against EGLE WRD investigation reports]). The source itself, however, was never fully eliminated: following the facility's closure and relocation to Howell (2024–2025 [VERIFY exact date]), Michigan's Attorney General filed a June 2025 civil action alleging that the shuttered Wixom sites continued to discharge PFOS-bearing stormwater, with one outfall reported at 220,000 ng/L (allegation; value and sampling location [VERIFY against the complaint]). The watershed's PFAS source forcing is thus bimodal in time—a large, well-dated point-source step-down in 2018, followed by a residual diffuse stormwater pathway in the post-closure period—a structure the calibration window must accommodate.

The scientifically pivotal observation emerges from this post-control regime. Despite the ~99% water-column decline, fish-tissue PFOS in the Huron system—Kent Lake in particular—has plateaued above the consumption-advisory threshold rather than tracking the water-column recovery (Endicott et al. 2025). This divergence has been attributed to contaminated bed sediment acting as a secondary, internal source that continues to supply PFOS to the food web after external loading was curtailed. The regulatory weight of this plateau increased sharply in June 2025, when the Michigan Department of Health and Human Services lowered the PFOS Do-Not-Eat fish-tissue trigger from 300 to 50 ppb (MDHHS 2025), making the binding constraint on this system fish tissue rather than water-column concentration. A dynamic, sediment-coupled, reach-resolved process model is the natural instrument for mechanistically testing whether sediment legacy alone can reproduce this observed plateau—the central motivation developed in Section 1.

### 2.4 Available PFAS observations

Ambient PFAS surface-water data for the watershed are drawn principally from the EGLE surface-water monitoring program, which maintains per-station aqueous PFAS concentrations (reported in ng/L) accessible through its PFAS Surface Water data service, supplemented by USEPA and Water Quality Portal (WQP) records. These ambient stations constitute the in-stream calibration spine of the present work and are assigned to their hydrologically correct channel reaches by the drainage-area-aware procedure described in Section 3.4; a test sub-watershed demonstration snapped 58 EGLE stations to the SWAT+ network at a median planar distance of 3.9 m, with the full HUC8 station set being larger. Fish-tissue PFOS observations—from the EGLE Fish Contaminant Monitoring Program (including Huron fillet sampling ongoing as of early 2026) and from the Kent Lake tissue-and-sediment dataset underlying Endicott et al. (2025)—provide the independent, regulation-aligned validation target against the 50 ppb advisory threshold. We note that the watershed's PFAS signature is multi-component (recent work reports PFHxA as dominant in the water column and PFOS plus emerging fluorinated compounds in sediment); the present model is restricted to PFOS in this first version (Section 1.4), and the fish-tissue and emerging-compound observations are reserved for validation and future multi-congener extension rather than v1 calibration.
