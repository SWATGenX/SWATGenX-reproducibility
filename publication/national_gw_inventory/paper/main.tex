\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Sustainable groundwater management requires a three-dimensional picture of the subsurface, yet across
much of the world that information exists only as water-well driller's logs in fragmented,
partly-digitized government archives --- a barrier most acute where agency-scale digitization is
unaffordable. Using the United States as a
demonstration, we (i)~compile an availability census of the $\sim$13.4~million wells in the 48
conterminous states, classifying each by how its lithology is published: a first-pass audit finds
machine-readable lithology in 8 states ($\sim$3.2M wells), but systematic probing raises this to 17 states
($\sim$7.0M); 13 states ($\sim$4M) publish it only as scanned images, and 18 ($\sim$2.4M) publish no digital
lithology (the wells are registered, the logs are not); (ii)~from the machine-readable states we harmonize
the $\sim$3.4~million wells carrying a log into an open inventory of nearly 24~million depth-resolved
intervals, plus structured hydraulics for $\sim$2.8~million wells, all from public endpoints, no
privileged access; and (iii)~introduce a reproducible, cost-bounded method that recovers lithology from scanned logs
using vision-language models. Validated against a state agency's hand-transcriptions, the method
reproduces material class at 91.5\% accuracy (Cohen's $\kappa=0.90$) and the coarse/fine texture that
groundwater models consume at 96.0\% ($\kappa=0.92$), at roughly 1{,}000 tokens per log. We report cost in
tokens, a provider-independent unit, and quantify what each state does and does not expose. Because the
method transfers to any jurisdiction with scanned well records, it offers a low-cost path to the subsurface
data groundwater management needs; data and method are released openly.
\end{abstract}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} groundwater; driller's logs; lithology; vision-language models;
subsurface characterization; data harmonization; hydrogeology

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Highlights}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item Much U.S. well lithology is in scanned driller's logs, not machine-readable data
\item We harmonize ~24M lithology intervals from public sources into one open inventory
\item Vision-language models recover lithology from scans at 96.0\% coarse/fine accuracy
\item Cost is about 1,000 tokens per log, using only public, no-privilege access
\item The method transfers to any region that holds scanned water-well records
\end{itemize}

\section{Introduction}
Continental and coupled groundwater models require the subsurface's vertical structure --- the sequence of
geologic materials and their hydraulic properties with depth. In the United States that information is
generated as a regulatory byproduct: when a well is drilled, the driller records a log of the
materials encountered. Hundreds of millions of such logs exist, but they are held by individual state
agencies in heterogeneous, often non-machine-readable forms. Interoperability standards such as
GroundWaterML2 exist to bridge these schema differences \citep{brodaric2018gwml2}, but a standard cannot
substitute for the data itself: no harmonized national compilation of their lithology exists. Every regional model therefore re-digitizes its basin's driller's logs by
hand. Our contributions are: an availability census; a harmonized digital lithology inventory from
primary state sources; and a reproducible, cost-bounded method to recover the scanned remainder, with
per-state coverage, accuracy, and cost.

This inventory is complementary to the United States Groundwater Well Database
\citep[USGWD;][]{lin2024usgwd}, which harmonizes 14.26M well records to a data standard of location,
purpose, status, well depth, screen depth/length, and well capacity --- but, by design, carries
no lithology and no water levels. USGWD is the national well-location layer; this
inventory is the subsurface-structure-and-hydraulics layer that USGWD omits. The two compose. Likewise,
national hydrogeologic frameworks such as the USGS Secondary Hydrogeologic Regions partition the
conterminous US into terrane units at the land surface \citep{belitz2019regions}; our depth-resolved
lithology populates the third dimension those map-based frameworks leave empty.

\subsection{Who needs this, and the cost of not having it}
\label{sec:demand}
Three independent lines of evidence establish demand. First, the continental-modeling community
states the lack explicitly. A flagship review notes that ``groundwater models require spatially continuous
and consistent datasets of the hydrogeologic properties that control the movement of water through the
subsurface,'' yet ``the identification of hydrogeologic heterogeneity structure and physical properties is
difficult because it requires subsurface borehole \ldots data that are often scarce''
\citep{condon2021}; \citet{degraaf2020} state that ``no consistent 3D lithologic, hydrogeologic or
hydrofacies data set exists'' at continental scale; and ParFlow-CONUS is described by its own developers as
data-limited \citep{oneill2021conus1}. Even USGWD---the harmonized national well-location
database---explicitly excludes lithology ``due to [its] limited availability'' \citep{lin2024usgwd},
naming the precise gap this inventory fills. Second, agencies act on the need: the USGS mined
$\sim$458{,}000 driller's logs for a single regional flow model \citep{arihood2009} and standardized
$\sim$14M state records across the 24 glaciated (northern-tier) states \citep{bayless2017}, and a federal land
manager (USDA Forest Service) grounds its Groundwater Inventory, Monitoring \& Assessment guide in
hydrogeologic-unit characterization while acknowledging that map-unit lithology ``can change laterally''
below mappable resolution. Third, and most telling, is duplicated effort: because no harmonized
layer exists, every team that needs subsurface lithology re-compiles raw driller's logs from scratch
(Table~\ref{tab:demand})---thousands to hundreds of thousands of logs per study, each in a bespoke
workflow. That recurring, state-by-state re-compilation is the cost of the missing layer, and the demand
this inventory answers.

\subsection{How continental models represent the subsurface today --- and why it is a gap}
If primary driller-log lithology is so sparse, how are CONUS-scale subsurface models built? Not from it.
Continental hydrologic platforms take one of two routes, neither using harmonized well logs.
(1) Conceptual collapse: operational water-balance models---the NOAA National Water Model
(WRF-Hydro) and the USGS National Hydrologic Model (PRMS)---resolve only a $\sim$2~m soil column and
abstract everything beneath into lumped, calibrated reservoirs with no geologic structure.
(2) Generalized, map-derived inputs: physically based models parameterize the subsurface from a
small, shared set of products derived from geologic maps, not boreholes. The flagship integrated
model, ParFlow-CONUS, resolves the entire deep subsurface as one \citep[CONUS1: 5 layers,
102~m;][]{maxwell2015parflow} to six \citep[CONUS2: 10 layers, 392~m;][]{yang2023conus2} broad units, with
conductivity assigned from the global GLHYMPS permeability map \citep[itself derived from the GLiM
lithology map,][]{gleeson2014glhymps,hartmann2012glim}, soils from SSURGO, and depth-to-bedrock from
\citet{shangguan2017}. Its developers state plainly that the model is ``limited not by computational
expense but by data availability, with a lack of detailed depth-to-bedrock and aquifer thickness estimates
at meaningful resolution,'' and is better viewed as a ``shallow aquifer storage model''
\citep{oneill2021conus1}. The lithology underneath these permeability products is itself a coarse, surface,
polygon map: the GLiM lineage descends from continental compilations such as the 262{,}111-polygon North
American lithological map of \citet{moosdorf2010}, whose authors showed that map \emph{resolution and
source alone} shift modeled fluxes by roughly $-59\%$ to $+38\%$ --- generalization with a measured cost.
The same few products recur field-wide \citep{fan2013watertable,degraaf2017,pelletier2016,hengl2017soilgrids};
the modelling community has itself begun cataloguing this shared-input homogeneity and its biases through
community model portals \citep{zamrsky2025gromopo}.

National geologic products are no better as a three-dimensional lithology source. The seamless USGS State
Geologic Map Compilation \citep{horton2017sgmc} is a surface polygon geodatabase. USGS
three-dimensional geologic framework models resolve depth but are regional: an inventory found 38 models
covering $\sim$49\% of the CONUS at a $\sim$4{,}170~km$^2$ median footprint \citep{usgs2023inventory}. The
one effort to mine bulk driller's records at scale \citep[$\sim$14M logs;][]{bayless2017} is confined to
the glaciated 24 states and outputs derived hydrogeologic-property grids, not a lithology layer. At the
opposite extreme, research effort yields exquisite per-borehole records --- full geophysical and lithologic
logs, even NMR-measured hydraulic conductivity --- but only at a handful of wells
\citep[e.g.,][n$=$2 and n$=$4]{twining2017ds1058,uhlemann2019eastriver}. Continental subsurface data thus
splits between coarse-everywhere maps and exquisite-nowhere research holes (Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum}); the
scalable middle --- millions of driller's logs --- is what remains unharmonized. That absence, not a
computational or feasibility barrier, is the gap this inventory fills.

A second, less-remarked barrier is one of access position rather than data existence. The
comprehensive prior compilations were assembled by or with the agencies that hold the records: Bayless
et~al.\ drew on full ``State-managed collections'' obtained as an inter-agency data request, not a public
download. For anyone outside those walls --- the modeling and broader science community --- the same
records are scattered across dozens of independent state portals with no common index, each demanding
bespoke reverse-engineering; what is a phone call between agency offices becomes, for the public, a
fragmented data-mining problem. We therefore frame this inventory deliberately around the public
pathway --- every record reachable through a public endpoint with no privileged access --- which is both why
our per-state point counts can trail an agency's internal totals and why a reproducible, outside-the-firewall
assembly is itself part of the contribution.

The lock is as often legal-institutional as it is technical, and California is the sharpest
exemplar. Its driller's logs were statutorily confidential under California Water Code \S~13752
\citep{ca_watercode_13752} --- releasable only to authorized agencies or with the owner's consent --- and a
UC~Davis analysis notes that until $\sim$2017 essentially all California well logs were privately held data
(well-attributed but secondary). They became broadly public only after the Sustainable Groundwater
Management Act (2014) and the Open and Transparent Water Data Act (AB~1755, 2016)
\citep{ca_ab1755}, once owner personally identifying information was redacted and the reports posted, so the
bulk dataset is only $\sim$8 years old. The same statutory, not physical, barrier recurs across states; the
data exists and is increasingly public, but it stayed locked for institutional reasons that newly
machine-readable methods (\S\ref{sec:triage}) are only now able to unlock.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Harvest --- and the heterogeneity it exposes}
State sources are ArcGIS REST (most), bulk file, or by-request. A registry-driven harvester paginates each
REST layer to FlatGeobuf. We encountered, and the supplement catalogues (Table~\ref{tab:modes}), about ten
distinct access modes. The central methodological finding is that the access mode is not
predictable from the portal type: an HTML viewer resolved to a structured server-side lithology table for
some states (OK, IA) and to scanned PDFs for others (UT, ID); a structured-looking column was 100\%
empty (KS); and a state booked as carrying one interval per well (IL) in fact exposed the full multi-interval
log through a hidden, born-digital text-layer PDF endpoint. Every source must be dereferenced and probed
before it is classified.

\subsection{Harmonization to the Wellogic schema}
Each state's native lithology is mapped to one canonical interval schema (\code{source\_id | state |
well\_id | seq | top\_ft | bottom\_ft | thickness\_ft | description | normalized}). Depths are retained
in feet (1~ft~$=$~0.3048~m), the unit of the source driller's logs. Adapters handle
separate interval tables, wide inline columns (un-pivoted to long), and multi-file bulk sources. Verbatim
driller text is always retained; a rule-based keyword map provides the controlled vocabulary
(refinable post hoc). The downstream goal is parity with the per-well hydraulic attributes that
MODFLOW-style groundwater-model construction consumes (horizontal and vertical hydraulic conductivity,
aquifer thickness, and static water level); §\ref{sec:gaps} reports what raw ingredients exist for that
derivation.

\subsection{PDF triage and extraction --- a full-population, cost-definitive design}
\label{sec:triage}
For the PDF-only states we do not run a blind full mine, nor rely only on sampling. The
classification step---does a log have a text layer?---is free (\code{pdftotext}) and needs no vision model,
so it can be run over the whole state. For each well's log we stream-and-discard: fetch, run
\code{pdftotext}; if a text layer is present, extract the lithology now via a free state rule-parser
(where the layout is a clean table) or a cheap text-only large language model; if the page is a raster
scan, the well is recorded to an exact ``needs-vision'' worklist and deferred. No PDF is retained.
This yields, per state, the harvested digital intervals, an exact count of vision-required logs, and
therefore a definitive cost (digital cost incurred $+$ vision count $\times$ measured per-log vision
cost) rather than an extrapolated estimate. Raster logs are read with a vision-language model (Gemini~2.5,
native PDF input, forced JSON schema), an approach grounded in a method base that matured only recently:
multimodal models now read text-rich and handwritten documents \citep{gemini15,gpt4o,qwen25vl,internvl,
liu2023ocrbench,humphries2024archives,crosilla2025htr}, and the ``documents$\rightarrow$structured
database'' paradigm is established in adjacent domains \citep{dagdelen2024}. Within hydrogeology, machine
learning has been used to predict lithofacies from borehole geophysics \citep{hammond2024lithofacies}, and
natural-language processing is only now being introduced to groundwater archives \citep{christenson2026nlp};
within geoscience more broadly, prior LLM work classifies already-transcribed borehole text
\citep{ghorbanfekr2025geobertje}. None recovers structured lithology from scanned driller's logs at scale. The shift that makes this tractable is recent: vision-language
models from 2023 onward collapse OCR, handwriting recognition, layout parsing, and semantic typing into a
single prompt-and-validate step, changing the extraction economics from per-log hand-transcription to metered
inference. The closest scanned-record prior art quantifies the pre-VLM barrier and clarifies our methodological
departure. \citet{ma2024lanl} extract well location and depth from 160 historical oil-and-gas records via an
OCR-to-text conversion feeding a text LLM (Llama~2): accuracy holds at $\sim$100\% on clean digital PDFs
but falls to $\sim$70\% on image-based scans, precisely where OCR breaks down. \citet{ghorbanfekr2025geobertje}'s
domain-adapted model (GEOBERTje) classifies already-transcribed borehole descriptions into lithology
classes, outperforming a GPT-4 prompt by 11\%, but never touches a scan. We differ on all three axes: a
vision-language model reads the scanned form directly (no OCR stage to fail), the target is the harder
material-by-depth lithology log rather than point attributes, and it runs over a multi-state corpus rather than
hundreds of documents.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Availability census}
Of $\sim$13.4M wells across 48 CONUS states, after systematic probing lithology is machine-readable
(free, no vision model) for 17 states ($\sim$7.0M wells), available only as scanned PDF logs for
13 states ($\sim$4.0M), and without public digital lithology for 18 ($\sim$2.4M)
(Table~\ref{tab:census}, Figure~\ref{fig:census}). A central finding is that the machine-readable category
is roughly twice what a surface audit suggests: an initial classification placed only 8 states in
category~A, but deeper probing revealed structured sources --- server-rendered HTML lithology tables (e.g.\
Tennessee, New Mexico, Vermont), bulk strata files (Texas), and born-digital report PDFs (Ohio,
Massachusetts) --- for many states first taken to be PDF-only, moving $\sim$3.7M wells from the vision-model
column to the free column. Classification is evidence-based and bidirectional: Oklahoma reclassified
PDF$\rightarrow$digital once its viewer was found to render a server-side HTML lithology table
(243{,}801 wells), whereas Kansas reclassified digital$\rightarrow$PDF once its \code{GEOLOGICAL\_FORMATION}
column proved 100\% empty. Category~C is
``no harmonized digital lithology,'' not ``no data'': a state-by-state regulatory audit
(§\ref{sec:scattered}) found that records in these states are public and almost always merely undigitized
(paper/county-health custody) or held behind an unwired portal, rather than legally sealed today. Outright
sealing of the report itself is now rare and was largely historical: California's reports were
statutorily confidential until their $\sim$2017 release (§\ref{sec:california}), and the main current
restriction we found is Delaware's withholding of public-supply well locations.

\subsection{Extraction triage: the LLM/VLM split and its cost}
An initial per-state calibration (n$=$100; Table~\ref{tab:calib}, Figure~\ref{fig:calib}) establishes the
routing split the rest of the evaluation builds on. A retrievable log is present for 87--100\% of wells
everywhere; what varies sharply is the vision-required fraction --- the share of logs that are
raster scans needing a vision model rather than born-digital text --- which spans 0--100\% and is
anti-correlated with e-permitting era: recent e-permitting states (IL, WI, FL) are mostly born-digital (the
free/cheap text route), while legacy paper-archive states (OR, NV, SD, ID) are almost all scans. Extraction
is faithful across all states --- driller shorthand transcribed verbatim, legibility flagged, and empty
arrays returned (rather than fabricated intervals) on blank logs. The definitive large-sample yield and the
token-metered cost follow in Section~\ref{sec:yield}, and per-well validation against agency transcriptions
in Section~\ref{sec:accuracy}.

\paragraph{Definitive cost via full triage.} Because classification is free, the full-population triage
(§\ref{sec:triage}) converts the sampled estimate into a definitive per-state figure while harvesting the
digital share for free. Illinois is the first completed case: triaging all 390{,}443 Illinois wells confirmed 0\% vision-required
(born-digital text layers), harvesting 2{,}198{,}466 lithology intervals from 382{,}245 wells
($\approx$5.8 intervals/well) at zero vision-model cost. A national projection over the $\sim$4.0M
scanned-PDF (category~B) wells routed to the vision model --- the raster remainder once probing has moved
the born-digital share into the free category~A --- is on the order of 4~billion tokens ($\approx$\$2k at
flash-lite); the binding constraints are wall-clock and normalization-vocabulary QA, not
dollars or storage (stream-and-discard avoids a $\sim$700~GB archive).

\subsection{Large-$n$ yield and hydraulic-field coverage}
\label{sec:yield}
We evaluated eleven scanned-PDF states (1{,}000--1{,}500 logs each; Table~\ref{tab:yield},
Figures~\ref{fig:yield} and~\ref{fig:hydraulic}). A single ``success rate'' conflates three distinct
outcomes, so we report them separately. Extraction yield --- the fraction of documents the model
actually read that yielded a log --- is 94.6\% pooled (11{,}806 documents) and 99--100\% for
most states: once a scan is legible, the model recovers a log essentially always, and it returns an empty
array rather than fabricating intervals when none is present. The recovery gaps lie on two other axes.
Document availability varies widely: 28\% of Mississippi and 63\% of Utah wells simply have no scan on
file (``no-PDF''). And one state is a genuine extraction outlier --- Nebraska (65.5\%) --- because
its modern registrations increasingly omit the lithology log (the temporal heterogeneity of
Section~\ref{sec:accuracy}); New York's apparent shortfall is instead transient server flakiness ($\sim$24\%
retryable fetch failures), not extraction. For the spatially/temporally heterogeneous states (MS, NE, NV) the
reported figure is from the dual-stratified run. We report cost in tokens, the reproducible unit,
since per-token prices drift and vary by provider: the VLM (raster) route uses $\approx$1{,}000 tokens per log

\subsection{Extraction accuracy: validation against agency transcriptions}
\label{sec:accuracy}
Yield (Section~\ref{sec:yield}) measures whether a scanned log is readable, not whether the model
reads it correctly. California permits a direct accuracy test: 86{,}653 wells carry both a DWR
hand-transcribed FreeForm geologic log and the original scanned report. We sampled 1{,}499 such wells, passed
each scan through the same flash-lite pipeline, and scored its output against the agency transcription as
ground truth (Table~\ref{tab:accuracy}). Agreement is high on every axis that matters: interval count
Pearson $r=0.94$ (means 12.9 vs.\ 13.1; MAE 0.91~intervals), depth-boundary $F_1=0.97$ ($\pm$5~ft; recall
0.98, precision 0.97), and 12-class material accuracy 91.5\% (Cohen's $\kappa=0.90$) over 516{,}317 compared
feet. On the coarse/fine binary that texture and conductivity models actually consume, agreement is
96.0\% ($\kappa=0.92$). Per-class recall is highest for rock/bedrock (0.96), shale (0.95), sand (0.92) and
gravel (0.90), and lowest for the rare silt (0.79) and boulders (0.77). Two residual error sources are
reported rather than hidden: ($i$) $\sim$6\% of wells split their log across multiple scanned-page files, and
our single-page comparison understates recall for those (a fixable stitching gap that produces the long tail
in depth-extent error); and ($ii$) part of the material ``disagreement'' is wording granularity --- terse
human entries (``Soil \& Rock'', color-only lines) versus the fuller scan --- rather than model error, making
91.5\% a conservative floor. Against the agency's own labels, the model matches coarse/fine texture
essentially perfectly and material class to $\kappa\approx0.90$, validating the recovered layer for
hydrogeologic use.

\paragraph{Sampling representativeness.} A 1{,}000-log estimate is only as good as its sample, so we
re-evaluated six states (n$=$1{,}500) under a combined spatial$\times$temporal stratification (a
$\sim$1$^{\circ}$ latitude/longitude grid crossed with completion decade) and tested whether yield is
homogeneous across strata. For a proportion, precision depends on the sample size, not the population: at
n$=$1{,}000 the worst-case 95\% margin is $\pm$3.1\% and the finite-population correction is $\approx$1.000,
so sampling 1{,}000 of $\sim$10$^6$ scans is statistically tight provided the sample is unbiased.
Three states (WA, OR, UT) are design-robust --- per-stratum yield spreads $\le$9~percentage points with all
strata mutually inside Wilson 95\% intervals. Two are not: Mississippi varies by geography
(north-west counties $\sim$50\% versus $\sim$86--89\% in the south, tracking per-county scan coverage), and
Nebraska and Nevada vary by age --- Nebraska's modern registrations increasingly omit the lithology
log (76\% recovery in the 1960s falling to 23\% in the 2020s). These heterogeneities, invisible to an
age-only or spatially-clustered sample, both justify the dual stratification and are themselves findings
about how within-state completeness varies. The stratified runs also separate extraction yield from
fetch reliability: success as a fraction of documents the model actually read is the design-robust
quantity, whereas success as a fraction of attempts also absorbs transient server failures (Washington's
apparent 77\% rises to $\sim$99.9\% of fetched documents once download flakiness is excluded).

\section{Discussion}
The contribution is the harmonized framework plus a reproducible, cost-bounded method --- not a finished
national mine. We need not download and process every log to deliver value: we provide the method, the
per-state cost/accuracy/coverage estimates, and the harmonized data we can already release. The same
harmonized lithology, paired with the pump-test ingredients that often accompany it, is the input
continental models currently approximate with GLHYMPS-class generalized geology (§1.1), offering a path to
replace coarse deep-conductivity units with observation-based stratigraphy where log density allows.

\subsection{California: a case study in locked-but-recoverable data}
\label{sec:california}
California concentrates both themes of this paper --- the data exists but is locked, and vision-language
methods newly unlock it --- and we treat it as a worked example. The California Department of Water Resources
hosts on the order of $\sim$1~million scanned Well Completion Reports (our enumeration of the CKAN
``WCR PDF Links'' resource returns 1{,}012{,}784; DWR itself typically states ``\,$\sim$800{,}000--1{,}000{,}000\,'',
so we treat the figure as approximate), each carrying the driller's lithology log. Yet only
$\sim$104{,}000 unique wells' lithology has been transcribed into DWR's structured geologic-log tables
(across overlapping format categories, not additive: FreeForm $\approx$87{,}633, Generalized
$\approx$22{,}260, QuickPick $\approx$6{,}800, USCS $\approx$3{,}071), leaving roughly 90\% of California's
per-well lithology locked inside un-transcribed scans.

Three compounding reasons kept it locked. (i) Legal confidentiality: the logs were sealed under
California Water Code \S~13752 \citep{ca_watercode_13752} and became broadly public only after SGMA (2014) and
AB~1755 (2016) \citep{ca_ab1755}, so the public bulk dataset is only $\sim$8 years old (\S1.2).
(ii) Manual-transcription cost: even the USGS GAMA program has hand-transcribed only $\sim$19{,}247
reports verbatim ($<$3\% of the corpus), basin-by-basin \citep{haugen2023_gama}, the barrier being handwriting
and heterogeneous historical forms that classic OCR cannot read. (iii) Aggregate-not-per-well, low ROI:
every major use collapsed lithology into smoothed percent-coarse or texture fields rather than per-well logs.
The USGS Central Valley Hydrologic Model obtained $>$150{,}000 scanned logs from DWR but used only $\sim$8{,}500,
as binary coarse/fine percent-coarse on a $\sim$1-mile grid \citep{cvhm_faunt2009,faunt2010}, following the
foundational drillers'-log texture method of \citet{laudon_belitz1991}; CVHM2 continued this lineage
\citep{cvhm2_marcelli2022}; the attributed-WCR releases carry $\sim$260--290k construction attributes but not
lithology \citep{borkovich2023_wcr}; and Stanford's airborne-electromagnetic work used $\sim$11{,}645 drillers'-log
sediment profiles only to calibrate airborne EM \citep{knight2018aem,kang2025aem}. To our knowledge, no
published work performs statewide, per-well lithology extraction from the scanned California WCR corpus, and
none applies vision-language models to it --- the per-well, full-corpus extraction our triage pipeline
(\S\ref{sec:triage}) is designed to make routine. As a direct test we streamed 1{,}000 randomly sampled
un-transcribed California WCRs through the pipeline: every report fetched as a scanned (raster) PDF,
97.2\% yielded at least one lithology interval (mean 7.85 intervals per log), at \$0.29 per thousand logs.
At that yield the entire $\sim$908{,}000-well un-transcribed remainder is recoverable for
$\approx$0.9~billion tokens ($\approx$\$260 derived at the current flash-lite list price) --- turning a
90\%-locked statewide corpus into a structured per-well lithology layer for the token-equivalent of a single
field day.

\subsection{Why the data is scattered --- a governance outcome, not absence}
\label{sec:scattered}
The fragmentation has a specific, documentable cause, and it is not that the data is missing or
withheld. Surface-water data is federally unified because one agency both collects and houses it: the USGS
National Water Information System, populated through the federal--state Cooperative Water Program, which
imposes a single measurement method and schema. Groundwater never
acquired such a home. Water wells are regulated by state law---there is no federal well-construction
or well-log-reporting standard---because well permitting rides on state primacy over water allocation,
itself split between riparian (humid East) and prior-appropriation (arid West) doctrines. The result is
fifty schemas at the point of record creation and, within many states, custody divided by agency mission
(geological survey vs.\ water resources vs.\ environment vs.\ health). Every federal attempt to unify the
records was structurally limited: the National Ground-Water Monitoring Network (SECURE Water Act) is a
voluntary aggregator that cannot compel reporting; the Safe Drinking Water Act regulates only public
water systems, leaving the tens of millions of domestic wells---where the lithology lives---outside any
federal frame; and the National Geothermal Data System (DOE/ARRA, 2009--2014) federated the already-digitized,
geothermal- and petroleum-oriented borehole holdings of dozens of participating state geological surveys into a
common schema---each survey publishing only its own pre-existing digital subset, of uneven completeness, not a
fresh digitization of all wells---proving the integration concept feasible before losing funding and going
dark \citep{ngds_sustain}. Crucially, none of these reached the shallow domestic water-well driller's logs that carry
the lithology: NGDS drew on state geological-survey archives rather than the water-agency well-completion
systems, ingested existing digital records rather than extracting the scanned backlog, and ran entirely
before the largest water-well report corpora were even released to the public (e.g.\ California's
$\sim$2017 unsealing, \S\ref{sec:california}). The barrier is therefore sustainability and scope, not
capability---prior efforts showed national harmonization works, but none targeted, reached, or outlived the
water-well lithology problem this inventory addresses.

Privacy is real but narrow today, and was historically broader. Modern state public-records law is
public-by-default; where records remain restricted it is the owner's identity (California Water Code
\S13752 now releases construction and geology while withholding owner name and address) or the well's
exact location (PLSS-grid or landowner-supplied coordinates; Delaware withholds public-supply well
locations for source-water security)---not the lithology. The important caveat is temporal: several states'
reports were fully confidential until recently---California's were statutorily sealed until their
$\sim$2017 public release (§\ref{sec:california})---so confidentiality was a genuine historical brake
on bulk extraction, now largely lifted, even though it does not explain the cross-state scatter
(which is federalism, above). Domestic-well permitting exemptions explain why some logs are never collected at all
\citep{maxwell2019}. Crucially, a state-by-state audit of the apparent ``no-data'' states found most to be a
harvesting miss rather than an absence: of eleven states initially flagged, seven expose completion reports
through a named public portal, and the genuine gaps (Georgia, Maryland, Rhode Island, West Virginia) are
paper/county-health records never digitized---public records obtainable by request, not confidential.
``No digital lithology'' therefore means not yet harmonized or digitized, essentially never
absent or legally sealed.

\subsection{Known gaps and shortcomings --- a living inventory, not a closed product}
\label{sec:gaps}
We catalogue current holes so users can judge fitness and later versions can close them.
Lithology coverage is partial: the open v1.0 release harmonizes $\sim$3.4~million wells into
$\sim$24~million depth-resolved intervals (Table~\ref{tab:inventory}) --- the harvested-to-date subset of
the $\sim$7~million wells the census finds carry machine-readable lithology. The remainder is recoverable by
the same pipeline; the scanned-only states are characterized by validated sampling (yield, accuracy, cost)
rather than fully harvested, and a few states publish no public log at all. \emph{Hydraulic ingredients are present but
uneven}: static water level and total depth form a broad backbone, but the pump-test fields needed to
derive conductivity are scarce --- specific capacity is recoverable mainly where yield and drawdown
co-occur (e.g.\ Texas, Arizona, Wisconsin). We separately audited the three derivation-readiness
inputs that govern whether transmissivity can be reconstructed downstream: land-surface elevation (to place
depth-referenced contacts and water levels on a common datum), measured aquifer-test transmissivity
or conductivity, and pump-test duration. The asymmetry is stark. Surveyed land-surface elevation --- reported
as-is, never substituted with a modeled DEM fill --- is bulk-available for eleven states, often complete
(Alabama, Iowa, New~Mexico at 100\%; Ohio~97\%, Kentucky~94\%, Illinois~86\%) but absent from most records
elsewhere because elevation, unlike the driller-observed depths and water levels, is not measured at the
wellhead. A well completion or construction date fares far better: twenty-two states report it, most
at 90--100\%, giving the inventory a temporal axis (record vintage) for age-stratified analysis. Pump-test duration --- the input that lets specific capacity be promoted to transmissivity
via the Theis/Logan relations --- is harvestable for Texas ($\sim$156{,}000 wells), Kansas, and Nevada.
But measured transmissivity exists in public bulk form for essentially a single state (Nevada,
$\sim$25{,}000 wells) and nowhere else at scale --- the strongest evidence that a national conductivity
layer must be derived from the yield/drawdown/duration ingredients rather than read off the records,
and feasible only where those ingredients co-occur. Normalization leaves a
minority of intervals as ``other'' (regional vocabulary); geometry is PLSS section-center for some
states; and the scanned extraction, although validated against agency transcriptions for $\sim$1{,}500
wells (Section~\ref{sec:accuracy}), is not yet run over every state's full corpus. Some states resist the
driller's-log model entirely: Colorado's subsurface data is geophysical (gamma/resistivity logs) and
aquifer-framework based, not depth-to-material logs. None of these is fatal; each is a documented,
improvable hole.

\subsection{Transferability beyond the United States}
The United States is a demonstration, not the limit of the method. The conditions that make U.S. well
lithology hard to assemble --- records created under dozens of independent jurisdictions, partially
digitized, sometimes behind document portals or legal restriction, and never unified --- describe the
global state of water-well data, and are typically more severe in regions with the least monitoring
capacity. The pipeline depends only on (i)~a public list of wells carrying a document link and (ii)~a
vision-language model; it needs no privileged agency access, no per-form templates, and no
country-specific training. The same triage --- born-digital text versus raster scan --- and the same
token-metered extraction apply wherever driller's or borehole logs are scanned, which includes much of
Europe, South Asia, Africa, and Latin America where national well records remain image archives. For
data-poor regions, where agency-scale manual transcription is unaffordable, a method whose marginal cost is
a few hundred tokens of inference per well lowers the barrier to the subsurface-data foundation that
sustainable groundwater management requires. We therefore frame the contribution as a transferable
instrument for groundwater-data democratization, validated at scale in one country.
